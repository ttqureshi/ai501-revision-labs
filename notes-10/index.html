The derivative is the limit of secant slopes

Notes §3 · Figure 1

The secant through \(P=(x_0,f(x_0))\) and \(Q=(x_0+\Delta x,\,f(x_0+\Delta x))\) has the average slope below. As \(Q\) slides to \(P\) it becomes the tangent, and its slope becomes the derivative:

\[f'(x_0)=\lim_{\Delta x\to 0}\frac{f(x_0+\Delta x)-f(x_0)}{\Delta x}.\]
\(x\)\(y\)

What to notice

  • For \(x^2\) the secant slope is exactly \(2x_0+\Delta x\): it misses the tangent by \(\Delta x\), so halving the gap halves the error.
  • The derivative uses only what happens next to \(x_0\). That is all the hiker in the fog can feel.
Check yourself

For \(f(x)=x^3\) at \(x_0=1\), is the secant slope with \(\Delta x=0.1\) above or below \(f'(1)=3\)? Predict, then set the sliders and read it off.

Answer. Above: \((1.1^3-1)/0.1=3.31\). The curve bends upward for \(x>0\), so a secant to the right is steeper than the tangent.

2

The gradient points straight uphill

Notes §4 · Figure 3

Stepping in a unit direction \(\mathbf u\), the rate of change of \(f\) is the directional derivative

\[D_{\mathbf u}f=\nabla f^{\mathsf T}\mathbf u=\|\nabla f\|\cos\varphi .\]

It is largest when \(\mathbf u\) points along \(\nabla f\) (\(\varphi=0^\circ\)), most negative along \(-\nabla f\), and zero at \(\varphi=90^\circ\), which is the direction of the contour.

\(x\)\(y\)

What to notice

  • Contours of \(f(x,y)=x^2+3y^2\). Click or drag on the map to stand somewhere. The red arrow is \(\nabla f=(2x,\,6y)\); the blue arrow is your chosen \(\mathbf u\).
  • The red arrow always crosses the dashed contour at a right angle, but it does not point at the minimum. Steepest is a local fact.
Check yourself

At \((1,1)\), which unit direction decreases \(f\) fastest, and at what rate?

Answer. \(\nabla f=(2,6)\), so the direction is \(-(2,6)/\sqrt{40}\approx(-0.32,-0.95)\) and the rate is \(-\sqrt{40}\approx-6.32\).

3

A Taylor polynomial is a local map

Notes §7 · Figure 4

The Taylor polynomial of degree \(n\) around \(a\) matches the value and the first \(n\) derivatives of \(f\) at \(a\):

\[T_n(x)=\sum_{k=0}^{n}\frac{f^{(k)}(a)}{k!}(x-a)^k .\]

Here \(f(x)=\sin x+\cos x\), the example from the slides.

\(x\)\(y\)

What to notice

  • The shaded band is where \(|T_n-f|<0.1\). It is centred on \(a\) and widens as \(n\) grows.
  • Far from \(a\), even a high-degree polynomial shoots off. This is why gradient descent, which trusts only \(T_1\), must take small steps.
Check yourself

Why is \(T_2\) exact for every quadratic function, however far \(x\) is from \(a\)?

Answer. A quadratic has third and higher derivatives equal to zero, so every term after the second vanishes. This is why the second-order prediction of a gradient-descent step on the delivery loss is exact (Notes §7.2).

4

Convex means the chord lies above

Notes §8 · Figure 5

\(f\) is convex if, for all \(x_1,x_2\) and all \(\theta\in[0,1]\),

\[f\bigl(\theta x_1+(1-\theta)x_2\bigr)\;\le\;\theta f(x_1)+(1-\theta)f(x_2).\]

With \(\theta=\tfrac12\) and \(X\) equal to \(x_1\) or \(x_2\) with probability \(\tfrac12\) each, this is Jensen's inequality \(f(\mathbb E[X])\le\mathbb E[f(X)]\).

\(x\)\(f(x)\)

What to notice

  • Wherever the curve pokes above the chord, the segment is drawn red. For a convex function you can never make it red.
  • Try the quartic with \(x_1=-1.4,\ x_2=1.3\): the hump between the two valleys rises above the chord, and that hump is what creates the second, worse valley.
Check yourself

For \(X\in\{1,3\}\) with equal probability and \(f(x)=x^2\), compute \(\mathbb E[f(X)]-f(\mathbb E[X])\). What is this quantity called?

Answer. \(5-4=1\), the variance of \(X\). Jensen with \(f=x^2\) says a variance is never negative.

5

Gradient descent in the delivery valley

Notes §9 · Figures 6–7

Each step moves against the gradient, updating both parameters at once:

\[\boldsymbol\theta\leftarrow\boldsymbol\theta-\alpha\,\nabla\mathcal L(\boldsymbol\theta),\qquad \nabla\mathcal L=\Bigl(\tfrac1n\textstyle\sum_i r_i,\ \tfrac1n\sum_i r_i x_i\Bigr),\quad r_i=\theta_0+\theta_1x_i-y_i .\]

The Hessian \(H=\tfrac1n X^{\mathsf T}X=\begin{bmatrix}1&2.5\\2.5&7.5\end{bmatrix}\) has eigenvalues \(8.35\) and \(0.15\): a long, narrow ravine. Gradient descent converges only if \(\alpha<2/\lambda_{\max}\approx0.2395\).

\(\theta_0\)\(\theta_1\)

What to notice

  • With \(\alpha=0.1\) the first two steps reproduce the table in the notes: \((2,\,5.6)\) then \((2.4,\,6.5)\), with the loss falling from \(214.5\) to \(2.82\).
  • At \(\alpha=0.2\) the path zig-zags across the ravine and then crawls along its floor (about 210 steps to get within \(0.01\)). At \(\alpha=0.24\) it diverges.
  • Tick centre the feature: the Hessian becomes \(\operatorname{diag}(1,\,1.25)\), the bowl turns round, the safe limit rises to \(1.6\), and \(\alpha=0.8\) arrives in 5 steps. Click the map to start elsewhere.
Check yourself

For \(\mathcal L(\theta)=3\theta^2\), which \(\alpha\) reaches the minimum in one step, and above which \(\alpha\) does gradient descent diverge?

Answer. A step gives \(\theta\leftarrow(1-6\alpha)\theta\). One step needs \(\alpha=1/6\); divergence when \(|1-6\alpha|>1\), i.e. \(\alpha>1/3=2/\lambda\) with \(\lambda=6\).

6

Batch, stochastic and mini-batch, for the same work

Notes §10 · Figure 8

With per-order losses \(g_i\), the three methods differ only in how many orders each step averages over:

\[\begin{aligned}\text{batch:}\quad &\mathbf w\leftarrow\mathbf w-\frac{\alpha}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\nabla g_i &&(n\text{ orders per step})\\ \text{SGD:}\quad &\mathbf w\leftarrow\mathbf w-\alpha\,\nabla g_i &&(1\text{ order per step})\\ \text{mini-batch:}\quad &\mathbf w\leftarrow\mathbf w-\frac{\alpha}{k}\sum_{i\in\mathcal S}\nabla g_i &&(k\text{ orders per step})\end{aligned}\]

Here there are \(n=40\) past orders (distance centred), so the noise has room to show. All three walkers read orders at the same rate.

What to notice

  • After one epoch batch has taken 1 step, mini-batch \(n/k\) steps and SGD \(n=40\) steps, for the same amount of reading.
  • In the lower plot SGD drops fastest at first and then flattens into a noisy floor; mini-batch gets close to SGD's speed with much less jitter. Raise \(\alpha\) and SGD's floor rises.
Check yourself

The slides write the mini-batch update with \(\tfrac1n\sum_{i=1}^{k}\). With \(n=50\,000\), \(k=32\), \(\alpha=0.1\), what step size does that really use?

Answer. \(\alpha k/n=0.1\times 32/50\,000=6.4\times10^{-5}\), about 1500 times too small. Divide by \(k\), not \(n\).
